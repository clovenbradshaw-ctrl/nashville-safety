=== PERMITLESS CARRY AND OFFICER SAFETY ===

Doucette, M.L., Ward, J.A., McCourt, A.D., Webster, D., & Crifasi, C.K. (2022).
Title: "Officer-Involved Shootings and Concealed Carry Weapons Permitting Laws: Analysis of Gun Violence Archive Data, 2014-2020."
Journal: Journal of Urban Health (Springer), peer-reviewed
DOI: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11524-022-00627-5
Key verbatim: "Permitless CCW adopting states saw a 12.9% increase in officer-involved shooting victimization rate, or an additional 4 OIS victimizations per year, compared to what would have happened had law adoption not occurred."
Design: Quasi-experimental, 10 states that loosened carry permitting 2014-2020 vs. 26 states that retained requirements.
Mechanism relevant to traffic stops: ambient firearm uncertainty raises confrontational risk — directly relevant to officer willingness to initiate discretionary traffic stops.

=== TENNESSEE PERMITLESS CARRY LEGISLATION ===

Tennessee HB0786, 112th General Assembly.
URL: https://wapp.capitol.tn.gov/apps/BillInfo/Default?BillNumber=HB0786&ga=112
Signed: April 2021. Effective: July 1, 2021.
Effect: Removes permit requirement for most adults to carry a loaded handgun, open or concealed.

Law enforcement opposition on record: Tennessee Sheriff's Association, Tennessee Bureau of Investigation, Tennessee Chiefs of Police all formally opposed.

Tennessee Lookout (March 2021):
URL: https://tennesseelookout.com/2021/03/17/commentary-fighting-myths-with-facts-why-permitless-carry-is-bad-news-for-tennessee/
Key verbatim: "Without the permit requirement, law enforcement has no way of differentiating between citizens lawfully carrying guns and people carrying guns who are prohibited from doing so by law."

Memphis Police Department information page on compliance:
URL: https://www.memphispolice.org/tn-permitless-carry/

Tennessee duty-to-inform: Tennessee has NO statutory duty to inform a law enforcement officer that you are armed during a traffic stop.
Source: https://www.ccwhub.com/states/tennessee/duty-to-inform
Relevance: Officers stopping a vehicle in Tennessee cannot know whether occupants are armed; post-2021 they also cannot assume only permitted carriers are armed.

GAP: No published study or MNPD internal data specifically measuring changes in Nashville traffic stop volume before/after July 1, 2021. The qualitative enforcement concern is documented; the quantitative effect on Nashville stop rates is not.

=== FERGUSON EFFECT ON TRAFFIC STOPS — PEER-REVIEWED ===

Shjarback, J.A., Pyrooz, D.C., Wolfe, S.E., & Decker, S.H. (2017).
Title: "De-policing and crime in the wake of Ferguson: Racialized changes in the quantity and quality of policing among Missouri police departments."
Journal: Journal of Criminal Justice, vol. 50, pp. 42-52
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrimjus.2017.04.003
PDF: https://www.utep.edu/liberalarts/criminal-justice/_files/docs/shjarback-et-al.-2017-jcj.pdf
Key finding: Approximately 67,000 fewer stops in 2015 compared to 2014 across 118 Missouri departments (-0.11 standardized change). Declines amplified in predominantly Black jurisdictions.

Force Science Institute (2016) officer survey:
URL: https://www.forcescience.com/2016/04/is-the-ferguson-effect-real-survey-says/
Key reported finding: "Nearly half of patrol officers surveyed nationally say they have cut back on traffic and pedestrian stops." 49% reduced proactive traffic stops by 5-10 per month; 47% reduced pedestrian stops similarly.
Caveat: Not peer-reviewed. Conducted by a law enforcement training firm. Methodology not published in journal. Useful for self-reported officer behavior only.

Pew Research Center. "Behind the Badge." 2017.
URL: https://www.police1.com/officer-safety/articles/pew-survey-officers-more-reluctant-to-use-force-make-stops-7Z1jq8dFPLpk5bV0/
Key finding: Large nationally representative survey of officers found widespread reported reluctance to conduct stops post-Ferguson.

=== FORMAL DE-POLICING POLICY AND TRAFFIC STOPS — PEER-REVIEWED ===

Boehme, H.M. & Mourtgos, S.M. (2024).
Title: "The effect of formal de-policing on police traffic stop behavior and crime: Early evidence from LAPD's policy to restrict discretionary traffic stops."
Journal: Criminology & Public Policy, vol. 23, pp. 517-542
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1745-9133.12673
URL: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1745-9133.12673
Key finding: After LAPD prohibited minor infraction pretextual stops (March 1, 2022), average weekly stops fell from ~5,439 to ~3,867 — approximately 1,572 fewer per week. Both violent and property crimes increased post-intervention. Property crime increase assessed as highly probable to be real.
Directly applicable to: policy-level traffic enforcement pullback effects on crime outcomes.

Nix, J. et al. (2024).
Title: "When police pull back: Neighborhood-level effects of de-policing on violent and property crime."
Journal: Criminology (published 2024)
DOI: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1745-9125.12363
Also: https://digitalcommons.unomaha.edu/criminaljusticefacpub/304/
Key finding: Using COVID-19 onset and George Floyd protests as exogenous shocks to Denver policing, reductions in stops and drug arrests associated with significant increases in violent and property crime. Traffic stops specifically: "officers made notably fewer traffic stops in 2020 — a huge departure from the norm — and the reduction had a much smaller relationship with changes in crime than pedestrian stops."

=== GAPS HONESTLY STATED ===

1. No peer-reviewed study establishes a direct link between political autonomy values and reduced traffic law compliance. That question is not empirically published.
2. No study specifically measures traffic stop frequency in Tennessee or Nashville before/after HB786 (July 2021).
3. No peer-reviewed study isolates police willingness to initiate traffic stops as the dependent variable under permitless carry specifically (Doucette measures OIS outcomes, not stop frequency).
